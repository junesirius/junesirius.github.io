It's a warm, sunny day, most of the days are like this in this part of the world. California, that is. The best part that's not too far from the trees and mountains, and close enough to smell the salt in the wind blowing through the open window facing west. Dean wonders why they didn't think of having a house and living here earlier. If there is a heaven, this is sure his Heaven right here.

There's a young man standing half-way through the drive-way, looking at the window when Dean gets up and opens the living room curtain. Sun leaves a comfortable patch on the carpet right near the window.

Dean frowns slightly, but doesn't give it too much thought. Maybe just a random wanderer accidentally lose his way, maybe he'd be gone soon.

But when he comes back to the window with a freshly brewed coffee Cas just made him, the young man is still there. Barely moving an inch.

Dean drinks the steaming hot coffee slowly, studying the young man's feature. He doesn't think he's ever seen him before.

"I can go ask if he needs any help," Cas offers him, from the seat near their dining room table, sipping a cup of coffee of his own.

"Nah, I'll go check him out. Might be some old hunter's boy or something. They should just just make a phone call." Dean leaves the coffee cup on the table and walks out in his slippers.

"Can I help you?" Dean asks as he gets a few steps down their driveway.

He's pretty sure he's not speaking too loud and scary, but the young man startles fiercely. He winces in a way as if he wants to turn around and run away, but he's using his will to pin his foot to the ground.

"Are you... Dean?" The young man asks carefully, searching his face. He almost still look like a kid, Dean notices as he gets closer. The baby's chubbiness is still hanging there.

Dean gives him a vague nod. "And you are?"

The young man's feet starts drawing circles on the cobbled road. He speaks with such uncertainty that almost look like he's not sure about his name anymore.

"Ben," he says finally, after fidgeting for a tad too long. "Braedon. Mom doesn't know I'm here -- in fact, I don't know where mom is. She's been missing for a week. And... I think either you're my dad, or you're the one who killed my dad, but I don't know which."

<hr>

Ben has always been bothered by his fussy memory before that car crash that changed their life. His mom couldn't remember anything from the past year, and even the two three years before that are vague whenever Ben asks her about some details. Doctors said it is sometimes normal after car crashes and head injury. The memory might come back after enough rest and recovery, but there's also an opportunity that those memories were just gone.

Lisa doesn't look so bothered, at least not as much as Ben.

The weird thing is, Ben's pretty sure that car crash only hurt his mom pretty bad, then it makes no sense why his memory becomes fussy too.

"Why did we move away from Indiana?" Ben has asked her. "I remember growing up there with a huge yard and many friends, why did we move to Michigan?"

Lisa would pause whatever she was doing at the time when Ben starts asking questions about his childhood, tilting her head to show she's really trying to think back but comes out with nothing. "Must be a job change or something, sorry honey, I can't really remember." She would rub her temple apologetically, giving him a tight smile and a little shrug.

"Why did we move away from Michigan then?" Ben would ask again, not satisfied with the answer. He asked the same question when he was twelve, then fifteen, then eighteen. Especially when he gets to a new middle school or high school or college, and he met some new friends, and when people starts telling their own childhood adventure stories, Ben would try to tell them his, except, he often comes up with a blank gap here and there. He couldn't remember who takes him to school when he was fourth grade, or who brings him home after his baseball training, or who was with him when he went out to the Halloween nights when he was ten. He was pretty sure it wasn't his mom, because he figures he'd remember if she did.

His mom shrugs again. "Another job change, I guess? And that awful car crash kinda made me want to get away from that state as a whole. It felts like a bad luck, you know. Why, baby, you don't like living here?"

Ben shakes his head, not knowing really how to explain. He's going to college anyway in a few month, no need to worry his mom again about whether he's happy. He is, in general, growing up with his mom, but there's also always a prickling feeling at the back of his head, like there could have been something more, something lost, something missing, something forgotten. But he doesn't know what it is.

More than once, Ben tried to ask about his dad. Especially when seeing some other kids have their dads watching and cheering for their baseball games, or going to the school's days, or having the whole family in their graduation ceremonies. But he also know if sometimes makes his mom sad when he asks, as if she's sorry for not giving him enough, not giving him more -- which totally isn't his point. He's just curious to know, he thinks, his mom is great and the best, but wouldn't that be greater and even better if there's a dad somewhere in his memory?

It is probably that chain of thought that leads to his slow correlation, and that theory gradually becomes more and more convincing in his head that he can't find a reason why it wouldn't be the truth: maybe that missing gap in his and his mom's memory, is exactly his dad. Maybe his dad used to take him to school and bring him home after his baseball training, his dad cook them breakfast with too much sugar and too little veggie, his dad making the Halloween costumes with him and going trick or treat with him. But maybe something really bad happened, he lost his dad and his mom lost the man she loved, maybe it's the same car crash that made them forget that took away his dad. And it was the pain and grief that made him and his mom forget.

That theory seems to make total sense, so Ben starts going with the story that he lost his father when he was young but it has been so long ago that he couldn't remember what his name is or what he looks like.

But that theory crumbled when he goes back to the old home. He got a summer job at Cicero, Indiana when he just fresh out of college.

The view and surrounding looks so familiar when the plane landed that he felt as if walking right into a long forgotten dream. He doesn't even know he still remember all the road and directions, but before his brain catches up, his legs already took him right back to his childhood house.

He can see the small differences that stands out from his memory - the door is painted a kind of sunset yellow instead of the white that his mom loved to keep clean, the grass in the yard is longer than they ever was when he was tasked to mow the lawn. Across the window, he can hear the sound of some young kid chuckling. He really doesn't mean to pry, but the wind blow the curtain a little through the opened window, and he can see a man maybe in his 40s or 50s, holding a little girl up over his shoulders, pretending to throw her over the shoulder before dropping her back down to the ground. The girls chuckles loudly whenever she looks like as if flying, but her dad is always there to catch her. There's an older girl and a woman sitting next to a dining table, at the exact same place in the diner room right next to the kitchen, where Ben knows exactly where it should be. Ben blinks hard, trying to stop the sting feeling in his eyes. His mom was great, but he couldn't remember being thrown in the air and had fun with a dad like he's seeing.

"Ben?" A woman's voice comes from the left of him, startling him a little.

The older lady stands in the neighbor's yard, looking at him with joyous eyes. Ben stares at her for a second too long before realizing, "Mrs. Fair!"

"Oh, come on, Stacy," the older woman chuckles slightly. "Look at you, you're not the little boy anymore. I thought it was you, but I couldn't be sure. It's been, what, ten years now?"

Ben moves a step or two closer, thinking back. "Sounds about right."

"How's your mom? Still kicking ass and hanging out with cool guys like she used to do?" Stacy teases kindly, her eyes sparkling in a way only a comic character is supposed to be able to. Ben remembers that exact look when she remembers some neighbors gossiping at a pool at his eighth birthday.

It is a little awkward to think though, really. But he's not the eight-year-old he used to be, and he knows it's just unharmful gossiping interest those ladies' been always sharing, with or without his mom know. So he just shrugs a little, "Sometimes, yeah. But no one sticks around." He says casually, as if it doesn't hurt to think bitterly without saying, *all of them have been mom's boyfriends, more of less, but no one is my dad*.

"How about Dean?" Stacy gives a pointed look. "Last time we saw, he's been pretty helpful around your house."

"Who?" Ben asks, honestly doesn't recognize the name.

"You know, the good-looking guy, freckles, cute smile." Stacy curls up the tip of her lips, smiling meaningfully. "Come on, kid, don't pretend you don't know who I'm talking. Didn't you guys move away to Michigan just to live together?"

"That was ten years ago." Ben says carefully, to confirm. *That was the time that mom and I didn't remember anything. Was there a man living with us in Michigan all those time?*

"Isn't time flying fast?" Stacy says in a nostalgic tone. "There was a time when I'd get invited to kid's parties, my girl would get crazy with games and I'd have a nice cup of drink near the pool with a sunbathe. And now, she's in the college and I'm just here in the backyard doing nothing."

Ben watches her closely, chewing his lips before letting the burning question slipping out from the tip of his tongue, "Is there anything else you remember about... Dean?"

Stacy gives him a funny look. "I thought you'd know him better than me, after all, all I heard is Lisa saying that was the best weekend of her life." She ticks her tongue for a pause, before starting again with a lowered voice. "And, well, I don't think it's my place to say, but since you're asking, and since you guys moved away to live together after, I figure you must have figured it out -- that crazy, semi-illegal weekend Lisa spent with Dean, was exactly nine months before your birthday. Hope you don't need me do the math for you now?"

Ben's head is spinning. His mom never said anything about Dean. There is no photo of the man in their home. But if what Stacy said is true, then this Dean... might be the exact answer of two major puzzles in his whole life -- the memory he doesn't remember, and the father he didn't have.

He stares at the woman before him, his tongue caught between his teeth. No answer comes out.

His face has enough answer on his behalf. Stacy watches him attentively, "You alright, hom? Surely it can't be the first time you realize that?"

"No," Ben hears himself answer automatically. "No, of course not. I'm fine, everything's fine. I just... I gotta go. It's been nice talking to you. Have a good day." He babbles rapidly, turning to his heels before hearing an answer.

He ducks into his car and breathes heavily. The world is still spinning around him. He still doesn't know what happened to their memories, there were still so many questions, but at least now he had a name -- Dean, the name of his father, possibly.

And if the neighbors in Indiana knew their whole family moved to Michigan, then maybe there will still be some old neighbors in Michigan that knows better about what happened back then?

Ben starts driving north.

Just like he thought, there are more people filling in the gaps for him in Battle Creek, Michigan. The old lady in the local grocery shop lets him know Dean would buy chocolate milk every week, apparently for him, and pies and beers, along with other "healthy food" whenever she recommend something is good for kids. The old man in the garage remembers him working there as a helper when he's not busy at the construction field. Though that construction site has long moved away because that apartment building has already finished construction and the building is already about ten years worn.

His old teachers and coaches remember Dean too, much to Ben's surprise. He was only thinking to try his luck, mostly just to pay a visit to the teachers, figuring there would be way too many parents in their life for a dad of a kid from ten years ago to leave a strong impression.

"Oh I remember him," one of his old teachers told him, "he's a fun person to talk with. I can see him in you."

"He's very proud when you won your first game," his coach told him with a short laugh. "I'm surprised you didn't remember that." Ben remembered that game, just not the part with Dean in it. He smiled anyway.

The bitterness on the tip of his tongue turns thicker and thicker, and his eyes sting more often than he would like. But so far he manages to not shed a tear in front of anyone, which he considered as a big win.

He drives straight back home, thinking, he'll have to have a talk with his mom. If his dad -- Dean -- was taken from them, at least he'll need to know -- he wants to know. Maybe the ten-year-old him couldn't handle the truth and had to block the whole memory away in his head, the twenty-one-year-old him is strong and grown enough to know what happened.

He comes back to an empty house. Mom isn't at home, and there is no note. Her phone is left at house, and there is no sign of anyone ever been at their home. The doors and windows are closed just normally.

Ben's heart sinks. His mom wouldn't just leave home like this without at least leaving a note or text to tell him or any neighbor where she's going, but it's also not the first time she left home and realize she forgot where the home was. Like those movies he saw, *The Notebook*, *50 First Dates*, *Falling*, too many stories telling the same sad reality: people forget, and for some, memories flow away like sand between fingers.

He didn't want to believe it the first two times it happened, after all, he still thinks his mom is way too young for the average age people getting Alzheimer's disease. But what does he know? He's not a doctor.

The third time he found Lisa wandering in the neighboring block, knocking on the door of an empty house that looks nothing like theirs, calling Ben's name, saying she forgot her keys, Ben had taken her to see a doctor. The brain scans look normal, according to doctors, but her memory is apparently deteriorating fast. She couldn't remember anything before the car crash ten years ago, but even for things after that, it was like watching a tsunami swallowing the beach bit by bit, corroding her head and Ben couldn't do anything about it.

Lisa tried to convince him it's no big deal, probably just because she didn't rest well enough for the past few days -- which, technically is true to affect the memory, but not as much as she's showing. The most likely explanation the doctors could think of is just, that car crash might have wounded her brain structure more than it was initially thought to. There might be invisible damage to the long-term memory division that, as she naturally ages, those damages become more apparent.

There is no cure for memory loss.

"I'm fine, Ben," Lisa said a hundredth time, rubbing his shoulder as if this can calm the now-grown kid down. Ben is promptly reminded of the time when he was a small boy and would get scared of something in the dark and his mom would rubbing his arms and back just like that. "Don't you worry. It's normal. Old people all have to handle that, more or less, right? I can just live with it."

"You're not *old*!" Ben pouts harshly.

Lisa chuckles. "Appreciate that, but I *am* more than forty now, after all. There was a time I thought I'd stay twenty forever, but then I wouldn't have you." Lisa looks at him fondly. "There's nothing I'd rather change."

"That was when you met dad," Ben remembers saying, dropping his head.

Lisa hums noncommittally. "Now, why don't you go on doing something fun and stop worrying?"

"I have to take care of you," Ben insists.

"I thought it's usually the other way round," Lisa answers.

"I promised," Ben suddenly remembers something.

"To whom?" Lisa raises one eyebrow.

"That man in the leather jacket that hit us, in that car crash," Ben frowns at the ground, trying to remember more details. What's that man look like? What's his hair color? His eye color? He only remembers himself thinking, that's a weird thing for the stranger to say, but he promised anyway. He wants to take care of his mom, with or without that stranger man asking him to, but now that he promised, this is something he just *has* to do.

"That was an odd thing to ask of you," Lisa comments, considering thoughtfully. "He was good-looking though," she sighed then. "Shoulda ask for a contact information, just, you know, things like this happen and I can at least give him a phone call."

"I doubt he'd be willing to give us any more compensation after all this time." Ben points out reasonably.

"At least I can call him a bastard and ask if he wants to buy me a drink," Lisa jokes lightly.

Ben fell into silence, back then. That actually wasn't a bad idea. Maybe there would be more records about the accident too, or maybe that driver could remember something more. If his dad was really there, gone in that car crash, at least there would be another person knowing him, remembering him.

But the old hospital couldn't find any record about the man, when he went to ask, only his mom's. There's no police report either. So there's the dead end again.

So they just let that topic drop.

Ben's comforted slightly only when his mom agrees to text him or leave a note in the future whenever she finds the need to leave the house alone, and always, always keep a fully charged phone with her.

<hr>

Which is exactly why Ben is fully panicking now.

His mom's usual phone is on the dining room table, with unread messages and phone calls from himself and some of her local friends -- and by the look of it, she's been gone for at least half a day now. She has taken the key and locked the front door. Her car is gone too.

The local county sheriff said she's only counted a missing person if she's confirmed to be gone for 24 hours. Ben felt he's one second away from yelling at the sheriff to his face. He couldn't sleep that whole night and went right back to the sheriff's office first thing in the morning.

There's no sign of her anywhere in the area.

Ben checked all the local places she'd usually go, even all the back of the buildings and dumpsters and homeless shelters that he usually wouldn't notice to pay enough attention. But he checked those places, checked anyone he could find to ask.

He printed out missing person notice for his mom, but he couldn't even really tell what color of clothes she wore. Security camera on the street only captured her car with a vague shape of the person inside at the first corner outside their home, and then the car just went out of sight -- you really couldn't expect small town CCTV system to be like big city level.

His mom isn't even meeting the requirement for the police to send out the standard Silver Alert for amnesic old people because she wasn't older than 65 years old.

The sheriff said he's already doing everything he can. But three days passed and no news about his mom, Ben thinks he's losing his mind.

He can't sleep, he can't eat, he can't even sit still for more than two minutes without standing up and start pacing in the living room again. The house is way too quiet for him alone, there should be more people here, his mom should be here, and his dad should be here. And if his dad is here, or even just if he knows his phone number, maybe he can call him, and he can be here for him, and look for his mom with him.

Ben crashes down on the diner chair and feels his throat tight, tears threatening to drop. He has the strangest impulse thinking, *if only Dean is here.* No, he means his dad, if only his dad is here. He corrects himself, but then recorrects himself, *if only that Dean guy is really his dad and he's here.* A drop of tear falls.

He stands up again, still can't sit for more than two minutes, wandering into his mom's bedroom, searching for anything that might suggest where she might go. There's an old magazine borrowed from local library on the bedside stand, folded to a page that shows a holiday commercial about sunshine and beach in California, and in the corner of the same page is another commercial, a black vintage car from fifty years ago for sale.

Ben looks at the commercials for a minute too long -- there's a glitch of memory flashing at the back of his head like a lamp on the brim of breaking down. There's something there underneath his memory trying to bubble up but can't quite make it. He thinks he sees a car of the similar color and model in his memory, when he was around ten, parked in their garage. But he knows his mom never own one like that and he can't figure out if that's a memory or something his brain just made up because of the commercial he just saw. He puts the magazine down.

The bedside drawer has some more personal things his mom owns. For a second Ben feels bad about looking into his mom's items, but a second thought overwhelms him by saying if he doesn't do this, he might never find his mom, and his mom could be in danger, even dead.

He opens the drawer. The first thing he saw is a cracked phone. The screen is cracked in the middle, broken glasses crawling to the edge like a spider net.

Another glitch of memory flashes by. He sees himself holding this exact phone, dialing the shortcut number, and crying into the speaker, "Dean, I need you. They had mom! What should I do?" He can hear his own ten-year-old voice in his head, and the dread and hope seep out from these words at the same time. He feels his fingers cold and heavy when they reach out to that phone.

He tries the power button but it doesn't turn on. It's a long shot anyway, not knowing if the broken phone even still works.

Ben still goes outside, to the corner of the living room where they kept the box of all chargers and cables, and find one that fits.

A blue circle shows up on the screen when he plugs it in. He forces himself to pace around the room for another ten minutes to allow the phone to at least charge a little more, before going back to try the power button again.

The phone turns on slowly. The screen sensitivity is awful and most of the times he can't click on the icon he wants to press, but eventually he manages to pull out the contact page, and there it is, on top of the recent phone call, the name reads "Dean", and the date is ten years ago.

Ben clicks on the name with shaky finger, his heart thumping heavily.

A robotic voice comes out, telling him the number is no longer active.

Ben hangs up, laughing out hoarsely. Laughing at himself for even having his hope up. Laughing at the child he's turning into again. For all he knows, Dean is dead, has been, for ten years. There's no chance that number is still active, even less, to come through.

He throws the phone back into the charging box, forcing himself to breathe, to think.

He walks back to his mom's room again, looking at that magazine again. Maybe his mom wasn't looking at that "impala" car, maybe she just saw that sunshine and beach image of California and thought that would be a nice spot for sightseeing? For all he know, that's just as much and as little chance to find her there than staying home doing nothing to expect her remember home and coming back herself.

Ben grits his jaw hard, looking around the empty, taking the magazine and the broken phone with him, thinking twice and then grabbing Lisa's usual phone that she left behind as well just in case. He sits back into his car, start driving west. He's going to find his mom.

<hr>

California is a big state, and there are way too many small towns along the beach with similar view like the magazine shows, and way too many houses in those towns. He doesn't even know if this is where his mom might be heading, but it at least gives him somewhere to start.

He starts from the southmost end where it almost reaches Mexico and works his way north. If he can take a second to enjoy the view, maybe he'd appreciate the sound of seagulls and salty smell of sea wind more. It is a really nice view, all things considered, if only he's not on a trip looking for his mom.

The small towns scatter slightly more sparsely as soon as he moves away from big cities like San Diego and Los Angeles, in between them, the small houses lay against the inland woods and forest, some almost bordering the reserves and national parks, others remain facing the beach and sea. Ben wonders more than once how much it must cost to have a house in these luxury places, living a rich man's life, but his thought never linger too long on them.

He takes a detour when he reaches north of Los Angeles, somewhere between Malibu and Ventura, some roads are closed for maintenance and he ends up wandering into a part he's pretty sure belonging to some national park. The woods turn deep and the altitude of the road keeps climbing up. His phone loses signal, and it's getting dark.

Temperature drops quicker than he expected, and he can't risk burning the fuel for AC if he wants to save enough to drive to next place with civilization.

Ben kills the engine when he can't see anything beyond where the car's front light shines. There's a backup jacket at the trunk that he doesn't know when he left there but it's more than helpful at this point. He climbs into the backseat, wrapping himself in that jacket, trying to lie down on the seat for some rest.

His stomach protests in starvation but he doesn't have any food in the car and he certainly doesn't want to risk going out into the woods -- his camping knowledge is only limited to camp site where there are lines telling you where is safe to camp and a small convenience store set up from a truck selling fast food.

The woods are louder than he ever realizes at night. Most birds are quiet after sun set where he lives, but here, there are so many hoots and shrieks and screeches. The wind sweep through the leaves, making loud shuddering sound as if storms are coming. He's pretty sure he heard some distant growling too, and his brain starts memorizing what's the most likely predator in the area. California is famous for black bears and mountain lions, if his high school ecology classes is anything to go by. And he decides if he really run into them, at least he'd hope the car would be enough of a protection -- or, rather, all the protection he could ever get.

He spends the whole night thinking, his mind swirling in the sea of thoughts, making him nauseous as if he's been sleeping on a boat. He's too worried about his mom, where she is now, is she safe, does she remember her name, her phone number, her way home, did she get some help from someone kind. And when that train of thought practically wrecks at every question he doesn't have answer for, he tries to distract himself by thinking something nice, something slightly better -- he wonders how life was like when his dad -- Dean -- was living with them. It must be happy, he thinks, since all those people he talked to said Dean was a nice, funny guy that obviously cares for him and his mom so much that everyone around them can tell, the grocery lady and the school coach alike. He doesn't have any photo of Dean so he has to fill the blank in with an imagined figure. He images him tall, with dark hair, his eyes sparkling with mischief and joy, his lips twitch when he tries to hold back a laugh on a silly joke and cracks when they realize it's only a joke. They would laugh, there would be so much laughter in the house. Mom would be happy, and he would be happy too. Ben doesn't realize when his tears fall and the wetness almost startles him. He didn't cry when he was worried sick about his mom, but he cried imagining the happiness he didn't have, or he had but he forgot (or he thinks maybe the young him chose to forget because it was too much pain to know he'd never have it with Dean again for reasons he couldn't know). It hurts more.

He wipes his eyes messily on the jacket he's pillowing on.

Wind continues to bellow outside the car like a monster waiting in the shadow. Ben is shaking on the backseat, but he tries really really hard to calm his heart that's beating way too fast.

"It's okay, Ben, you're okay," he talks to himself, imagining the voice comes from the imagined Dean. "You'll be fine. I'll take care of you."

He imagines the man that might be his dad strong and fearless. He'd feel safe wherever he is, as long as Dean is there -- isn't it what most people feel at the presence of their father? Ben wipes his eyes again, harder this time. He doesn't know how that would feel like, safe, because in his memory, he never had a dad, or a father.
