Surprisingly better than I expected. Wasn't feeling very optimistic just by looking at the cast list, and unsurprisingly the cast is probably the worst part of the movie, but everything else is so unexpectedly good. Read from somewhere and I agree: "If Nolan didn't get Oscar from Oppenheimer last year, he should get Oscar from The Odyssey, and The Odyssey deserves that Oscar more than Oppenheimer." (I almost fell asleep watching Oppenheimer last year and stopped having interest after about two hours, I'm sleep deprived watching it today and almost expected myself to be sleepy and maybe needing to fight to keep awake, but turns out not sleepy at all and the story manages to grasp me the whole three hours' time, so that's definitely an improvement.) But I'm sure he'd still get *some* Oscar from The Odyssey anyway.

### Casting

Come back to the movie, I have to bitch about the cast because it would kill me not to spit it out: It's just way too many type casting for my likes, like, everyone is almost exactly looking like they get the part because they had some similar and well-known, well-loved character they have played before. As long as they fit into that same type, they'd be doing okay. And maybe they *are* actually doing great/not so bad in their roles, but I wouldn't know, because just seeing their faces and knowing who they are and what they have played before is way too distracting and not helpful for the immersive movie experience, which totally doesn't help focusing on the story itself. So here's the type-casting I can just point out with my toes:

- Matt Damon: Once again someone who struggles to get home, suffers so much and finally did (*The Martian*); once again someone who doesn't remember who he is and has to remember his past slowly from flashbacks and retellings (*The Bourne Identity*)
- Tom Holland: Once again some young boy who wants to prove he's grown man and can shoulder the responsibility that he's actually not ready for and makes the grownups around him suffer for his mistakes (*Spiderman*)
- Robert Pattinson: Once again some man who is so attractive that women would just fall in love with out of no reason other than his pretty face but turns out he's a different man underneath the surface (*Twilight*)
- Zendaya: Once again some beautiful, wise, mysterious woman who is capable of giving lost man directions and advices and lead them somewhere they need to go (*Dune*)
- Jon Bernthal: Once again some tough soldier who just comes back from the battlefield, tired of fighting but can't ever stop, trauma from the battle field, main motivation being his wife and kids and family (*Punisher*)
- Anne Hathaway: Once again some woman who doesn't want to be defined or told what to do, stubborn enough to choose her own way (*Princess Dairies*, *Devil Wears Prada*)

So of all the stars that I know the name of, probably Anne Hathaway is slightly least type-casting because her type is pretty wild, all things considered. But it doesn't help that I know her well enough that watching her making out with Matt Damon and almost kissing Robert Pattinson is too jarring. And can't stop feeling too odd that she's already about the (Hollywood) age to act someone's mom, when, in my memory, she's still the kid herself. The saying has been true that Hollywood is cruel to actress, that as soon as you're not young and beautiful, there would be so many other younger and prettier ones coming up everywhere to replace you, and all that you can cast is just someone's mom, maid, not even someone's love interest, random sacrifice and collateral damages, civilians and passers-by, those characters in the corner of the screen that audience wouldn't even notice. It's the cruel reality of the Hollywood, and I just had to keep thinking that every time Anne Hathaway's face shows on the screen. She's just forty and some, and Tom Holland acting as his son is twenty something; Jensen was twenty six when Jeffrey Dean Morgan playing as his dad at thirty four; guess the age difference and gender really doesn't matter that much.

Anyway, being too familiar with them, them being too famous, I can't see the characters on the screen, only the actors, and it's the worst any casting could ever do.

### Story

Love the story a lot.

Love the angle of how the story is told. Being a such long and rich adventure story, it's easy to lose the story (and audience's attention) if you just show one danger and then next (which would be more suitable for series than 3h movie, which is too long already; a thought: why isn't there an Odyssey series? Feels so very easy to adapt), so they'd have to find the angle, and Nolan's angle is about Zeus's law, the law of how do you treat guests, the one rule that's repeated over and over, until finally you realize, the wrongness in Troy victory, is exactly what Zeus's law against. The Troy horse was a *gift*, and they exploited how a gift is received and celebrated and kill the city. It's rare to see the post-war trauma from the winner's eyes, but even as they won, they lost, and that flashback shot when Odysseus watch the fire burn, the horse leans on the city wall, the sculpture's head cut off and rolling down (which is also such a good shot in storytelling and cinematography), the trauma and pain and shock of it is so vivid. The shot is slowed down, the music so transcendent, the eyes and the looks in those eyes, the wavering fire, the muted yelling and screaming and killing. It's all so deep and strong for the feelings and emotions to come through. And right there, with Odysseus, I get it too. It's wrong, they won the war the wrong way, it's a gift. It's a *gift*. The Troyans were massacred by a *gift*. Oh so wrong and tragic and painful that is. That's why there have to be penance, that's why there have to be punishment and Zeus's fury, because they did wrong and now it's time to pay.

Totally didn't remember what the original *Odyssey* story/book is about, or maybe I never knew, maybe I never read, or maybe I just forgot, not sure which, but when the movie comes to the part with the one-eyed giant, that's when I suddenly realize it's a historic fantasy, not just a history. I'd known there're gods and children of gods and people like Achilles in that era, but I forgot that means magic and magic creatures and animalistic descendants of gods too. And the creepy scene of the one-eyed giants and the sheep flock in the cave is the first thing hit me in the face to remember that.

And then one island after another, more adventurous stops in his journey home, almost instantly fit into my very ancient childhood memory of that "Sinbad the Sailor" from "One Thousand and One Nights" (I wouldn't have known this is the series name or where it comes from if not for the encouragement to give it a try to search for it, and based on my very vague childhood memory that it's a seven-night journey with one stop being an island that turns out to be a whale, I *actually* found it; and I had no idea it's from "One Thousand and One Nights", but it makes sense), it's the easiest way to write an adventurous story, with each island and each encounter a hazard. But each stop is also different to be interesting.

The walking trees reminds me of the scene in "Sleep No More", which is originally supposedly from "Macbeth"; the witch turning men into pigs, that scene is just so creepy and intense, almost too scared to watch, reminds me too much of "Spirited Away"（千与千寻）(forgot this scene is exactly like the original book, but still managed to guess all the creatures on the island were actually human before it's revealed, it's creepy as hell but still so good to have captured the suspended biggest secret before it's revealed); the zombie scene, I didn't know it's supposed to be Odysseus going to Hell, but dead men coming to Earth is so intense, kinda like "Pirates of Caribbean" (the immediate next scene with the whirlpool and their sharp turn more so), but love it so much that it's more about emotional conversation than the creepiness, there are just so much emotion in every scene, so sharp, so deep, so much; the siren scene is well known enough, but I love the take in the writing, that what he heard is just the truth that he doesn't want to admit or face, that it's him not ready to go back home, it's him not really wanting to go home, it hurts and it's scary to know the true heart isn't what you thought you wanted, but you should stop lie to yourself to make the life easier, I would know.

Love the part that he pretends to be a beggar to get into the city and meet his wife, but he can't expose himself yet, so he said, he's not home yet. He's physically home, but he's not ready yet, he has things to do, works need to be done, so he's not home yet. He wouldn't be until he's ready and done. I feel him. It's so emotional again.

When it's near the ending and Odysseus is stringing the bow to prove he's him, I almost expect he'd fail, because that would make the story so very deep. The person who could string the bow was the one and only past Odysseus, and now, twenty years later, the man coming back is not that man anymore, he might still be Odysseus, but he's not the young, past Odysseus, he comes back a different man, and he's older. So this different, older man maybe wouldn't be able to string the bow, because he *is* a different person now. (But of course the story has to let him string it successfully, let the wife recognize him, let the guests gasp, let the fighting and killing begin.)

His son recognizes him from the kind way he treated his old, sick, poor dog; his maid recognizes him from the scar on his feet; his wife recognizes him from the way he can string the bow; his blind fighting/training friend knows it's him because he told him. Everyone recognizes him in different ways, from the way they used to see him and still sees in him, but also only in small tiny aspect, like a reflection of a kaleidoscope. Who could really see him as a whole and as himself?

The only part that really confused me for quite a while is, who is Calypso and where's she coming from and what does she want. Because of "Pirates of Caribbean", sorta guessed Calypso must be some sea god of some kind, not exactly a human, maybe just so happened to live alone on that island when Odysseus got washed ashore, and the guessing isn't too far off. She's a nymph, but I wasn't expecting her to fall in love with Odysseus and the whole capture and captive thing is just her way of trying to keep him to her, a love thing -- looks like the original version should have been she just capture him to torture him or something dark. The adaptation really makes the whole tone of the story calm and slow, despite how intense each adventurous stop gets to be.

The ending of the story, I don't know if it's original and adapted (as I was told it changed the original ending), it has left me with some questions, it's not very satisfactory, but it's also not so bad. I think most people rate it as okay-ish/not bad/acceptable is largely because of the ending (there should be a survey about overall rating of a movie and its ending, a good ending could have such huge affect on people's likeness of it, and so could a bad ending, even if the rest of the story is good/bad). I was still thinking the young prince would have needed his parents once he has his coronation, bet he wouldn't be happy that he'd have to do this alone now; but the part the couple just decides to start wandering in the world like a honeymoon, even though it's an exile (technically a self-induced exile that no one would have insisted if he doesn't want to do it to himself), it's a little hopeful, a little free, and it feels good. So it's quite conflicting, but overall not too unsatisfactory. The balance here is like walking on the tip of the blade. It's not easy to do it right (it's damn hard and Hollywood movies haven't had a decent or brilliant ending for a while, only that Project Hail Mary is one), but Nolan did it okay, so guess I can give him some credit.

### Technicality

#### Visual

It's Nolan, and it's high-budgeting movie, and of course the visual has to be incredible. I wonder which sets are really on the location and which ones are set, knowing it's Nolan, would guess probably the majority is actually the real world, real island, real old town cities. And everything just look so real and solid and beautiful. The set is awesome.

The cinematography is needless to complement. But I'll have to complain, because it's being so good, all the lights are probably real lights and flashlights, that it almost blinded me. The scene when they got "the fury of Poseidon along with Zeus" (which is basically thunderstorm and violent sea waves), the whole scene used so strong flashlights every other second, alternating the visual from dark and nothing visible to super bright and you get to see what's happening for a split second before going dark again, reminds me so powerfully about the flashing light scene in "Sleep No More" again, and it's actually painful for the eyes to see, it has to bear the so bright flashing light in order to capture that moment's actions and images and what's the story going, and then immediately dark and all one can do is hearing the sound, and then next flash.

I blame (partially) Imax for that, maybe next time watching someone so intense and good and iconic and big, I might just go with standard digital halls to avoid the suffering.

#### Sound

And that's the other thing I blame Imax for: the sound is deafening. The only other place I feel my ears suffer this much is in my metal/rock band concerts, and I'd learned my lesson to bring ear muffs with me, but I didn't think I'd need them in the movie theatre as well. The battle music is too loud, the escaping scenes are always too loud (and happen too many times, they ran toward their ship in the same panicking moves in at least three islands in a row: the first one-eyed giant, the second one with moving trees, the witch's island), and every time when our heroes are running for their lives, I have to plug my ears to save it from suffering.

I predicted at the beginning of the year about how many awards "Project Hail Mary" could get, but based on one huge assumption: those other movies coming out the rest/end of the year isn't as good or better in those categories, and The Odyssey is among those assumed rivals. And I'm right, The Odyssey is definitely going to compete, in best movie, best director, best visual, best scoring, best sound effect, best adaptation, and some.

I wouldn't be surprised if The Odyssey ends up getting higher place in best scoring/sound than Project Hail Mary, but personally I still favor Project Hail Mary's music so very much more.
