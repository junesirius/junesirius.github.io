It is so good.

OMG My mind is literally blown away.

It's astonishingly good.

It's a crime that I've never seen it before until this day.

It's unbelievably, breathtakingly good.

It's the best movie I've seen for a very very long time, maybe ever.

From the storytelling, to characters, to scriptwriting, the cinematography, the score, the ending, the emotions, the tension and attractions, every actor's performance. There is not a single thing that's not perfect about this movie.

The black-and-white is just the ultimate perfect color palette for this movie. Colorization has no way of making it better than it is.

Knowing the movie is set in 1941 and came out 1943, a WWII movie coming out even before WWII is ended, the historical value of it is even more mind-blowing.

It is absolutely breathtakingly amazing.

Want to immediately rewatch to capture the major things I felt during the first watch. Actually, wanted to rewatch from beginning already after maybe ten minutes in. Already knew it's an astonishingly good movie. It's so good that I even run out of words to say how good it is, it's just, *good*. No other movie is ever so good.

And the ending!! Especially the ending, is making the movie elevated to a whole new level. It's already good in every single aspect, but the ending makes it extraordinary. The modern movies and shows have become so parsimonious of giving a good ending, and even a good ending can sometimes feel very unsatisfied, let alone all the other deliberate bad unsatisfying sad endings. But this ending, the slightly sad but reasonable and hopeful ending for all our main characters, even the farewell between Rick and Ilsa doesn't feel so sad, "We'll always have Paris", and then Rick and Louis have "I think this is the beginning of a beautiful friendship", Laszlo knows Ilsa still loves him (or at least Rick makes him believe it for his sake) and can continue to fight for the freedom. Everyone gets the ending they deserve and the best one for all of them, either personally or for the bigger picture, and even if they (in movie or outside, characters or filmmakers) don't know yet about the result of the war, all of them are so full of hope. The hopeful ending becomes the most powerful, beautiful, amazing, memorable ending ever. The hope is so much better than a single deterministic good or bad ending. So here's the ultimate answer to my very long-term question about how to write the ending: you don't need to give answers to everything, give hope. There is nothing better you can give to the characters (and readers/audience) than hope.

Now here's the rewatch and deep-dives.

Even a thief character can be so memorizing. Five minutes in, the pocket-picker character comes in, picking the pocket and stealing the wallet. Three sentences of dialogue pretending to talk about what just happened but actually stealing, it's so funny but the dialogues are just so smooth that it's just so seamlessly perfect for the plot. (And we'll see him a few more times down the road, just to know there're all kinds of people here in Casablanca.)

First time we hear about Rick is from the Germans and Captain Renault, "Everybody comes to Rick's," and the Germans have already heard of Rick. The casual dropping line already starts setting up for Rick.

And the second implicit mention is from the ladies at the table, and the dropping line of joke about the second largest banker in Amsterdam and the biggest banker is now the pastry chef in the cafe. Doing so absolutely perfect in setting up the character for Rick. Even before meeting Rick, we already know Rick has to be the main character for this movie. The snobbishness and all these rules and titles, almost exactly like how *Butch Cassidy* introduces the character, it's just so perfect. No wonder the screenplay class has taught: the best way you set up a character, is through other characters' eyes, and they're doing exactly that, in the most perfect way.

And then, when the shot comes to Rick himself, the very first thing we see is his hand, signing a check, and then slowly pan up to his very indifferent face. The whole shot slowly panning up revealing his face from his hand, it's storytelling by itself. We get to know his power (at least here in the cafe), the respect everyone is giving him, the harsh demeanor, the hard shell he's wearing (which very apparently for cinematic effect will have to crack for the contrast). It's a powerful shot.

Also almost forget to mention, the first scene about Rick's cafe, the introduction is through Sam's song. The blue and jazz is just so beautiful, want to go to a retro blue bar immediately now.

The conversations afterwards, actually every single dialogue in this movie, contains certain subtext. I remember some screenwriting textbook mentioning how important and effective subtext is, how much better to leave things unsaid than spelling them out, probably *Casablanca* is THE best example of subtext.

Love the short exchange scene where Rick doesn't let the German comes into the gamble room. It's just a minute-long sequence, but already so subtly set out the tone showing his side. He's never neutral, no one in this world, under that war setting is ever really neutral.

Love the scene between Rick and Ugarte. The cynical comments is one thing to fall in love with ("You despise me, don't you?" "If I gave you more thought, I probably would." in such an elegant indifferent way), the very clever subtext-heavy conversation is another thing to be fully impressed by either how good the screenwriting is, or how elegant and clever people of that era actually are (and have to be). It's the most powerful moments when a strong man is vulnerable, when a royal man is hesitating, when a weak man turns strong, when a character gives up their usual attributes and becomes something they are usually not. It's those moments that we see the deep down real them. And here it is, the hard-shell, poker face Mr. Rick, has his gentle moments. Those playful lines with Sam, not going to bed ever, what time it is in New York when it's December in Casablanca, the nonsenses, the playfulness, the drunkenness, but also, a sense of letting the guard down, knowing it's only Sam here, and he doesn't have to worry or pretend when he's in front of Sam, he's safe to be not elegant or cautious or diplomatic, he's just being himself, being his young self. He's the real Richard when he's not under the burden of the war and responsibility. He's in pain, but he's also flesh and blood. It's so beautiful to see.
