wasn't lying when I say I can rewatch *Phantom of the Opera* infinite times, I really did mean I could watch it infinite times. It's like my weakness. wouldn't call it obsession because I consider myself not *crazy* about it, like I'm *not* exhilarated about watching it, but I also have zero self-control in terms of buying tickets and going back over and over again. Maybe I am obsessed, just in a new form I haven't experienced with any other show or play before.

It's the feeling and emotion that got me hooked. I just realized several days ago everything is about feeling, the concert is about feelings, the stories and writings are about feelings, and of course the musicals are all about feelings too. And I go back, over and over again, to feel the feelings. To feel the pain and torn and desperation, to feel hurt and ache and yearning, in a safe space; to allow myself tear up; to feel, as if I haven't been feeling everything, feeling way too much, already, all this time, all these days. The obsession is like licking the hole when a tooth just got removed, licking the aching dent, the place where something could have been but not anymore, licking and self-reminding over and over again about themes like love and want and pain and loss and grief. There is no other show like *Phantom of the Opera*. So I go back again.

The top of the hill view today isn't the best to feel the feelings, honestly. I can barely see much, certainly not the facial expressions like I did last time, which is probably (exactly) why I didn't get hit so hard when Christine ran back to return the ring -- I could see her cover her face before she ran back to Raoul, I couldn't see the grimace and pained face that I knew she would have, because that's what hit me last time. But this time, I got the whole stage view, and I got the feelings from the music. The music is still epic, and if my amateur ear learned anything from all this time of rewatching and relistening, I'd say the singers actually did a better job than last time in Costa Mesa. The Phantom's voice is in a better condition, and all the songs and sound effect sounds so much better, less tired, more powerful.

Then I see the story structure too. It's funny how all sources of information could affect me so easily. The worldcon taught me about the story structures, and here I was, sitting in the theatre, unconsciously started analyzing the story structure in my head. It's not like I hadn't done it before already, but today, it's running more automatically until it gets into the consciousness. So many musicals love the form of telling the story backwards, starting from the ending so you know the story has already finished, there's already an ending, but that doesn't make their past glory any less heartbroken, any less epic. *Mozart* did it, *Elizabeth* was like that too, and then there is *Phantom of the Opera*. When the most epic music of the main theme starts at the end of the auction, when the grey chandelier rises over the audience seat, the light brightening, the glory of the old time returns to the stage, like time rewinding back, it's hard not to have blood rushing to your head, sending thrills all over your body. It's a magical moment. It's a magical technique. So very neat and clever, and effective in the musical storytelling. I wonder if all those musical writers have learned it from the same place or they just learned from each other. All those musicals happen to be about historical events or people too, even though some may be fictional, they still seem historical and realistic enough to pass for a historical figure, and telling the story from the end is just about the best way of revealing that long forgotten history. Love the form so much.

And then there's the show-within-the-show format, the nested shows, as they call it in the story structure, the same as "nested story" structure from "One Thousand and One Nights." So very clever and immersive, so very easy to show different stage effect and performance and style, so volatile, making sure the audience has the very best immersive experience and never gets bored. Even the so called fill-in little episode of the ballet dance fits into the nested story so well for double effects. It's just genius.

And then there's the very absolute genius stage transition design. Like the scene when Christine first met Raoul and had the conflict moment of whether to follow him to a dinner or to take Phantom's singing class, the left side of the stage is the ballet dancers having their training lessons with the Mistress, and all those dancing just fit into the singing so well. Of course they're meant to be the dance-along for the atmosphere and emotions, but they also fit the plot need, had a perfectly reasonable explanation of why they even need to be there in the first place. It's just so clever and so well played out. It's a genius design.

And all those on-stage costume change and stage setting change, I can't appreciate enough how fast those shifts can be and all just played out so well. In the first act Christine changed from the ballet dancer's dress to the fancy solo singer's dress right before everyone's eyes, and once again did the same when she finished the singing and changed into the white night gown. The beautiful, elegant, smooth change doesn't break the narrative and doesn't require her to go off-stage, keeping the story flowing just so naturally, all the transition serves the story perfectly. It's so beautifully played out. It can't be more elegant and well-designed. And no other art form can do the same or have the same effect. That's exactly why any movie adaption, no matter how true to the script to the theatric design they try to be, they aren't the same, and they can't have the same emotional effect -- even those unnoticeable stage change is a part of the storytelling and therefore related to the emotions and feelings the story wants to convey. Noticed that the first time with *Wicked* musical vs the movies, and even though I can't remember much about *Phantom*'s movie (which, I definitely have to rewatch, as well as the original novel), I know as a fact that whatever the movie did, it can't be as good as the show.

The more times I rewatch this, the more clear I can see that there is no romantic love between Phantom and Christine, but it doesn't change that in its core this is a love story. There is love in it, just not the traditional romantic love. They each seek different things. For Christine, the love comes from the grief and missing her father, her entire feeling and hope and dependence on Phantom, on her angel of music, is because she lost her father and she missed him so much. Her father told her the angel will watch over her, and that's when Phantom showed up. Call it manipulation or serendipity as you please, Christine's feeling is clouded by the grief and the need of a father figure, and Phantom fits into the figure she needed him to be.

And Phantom needs and wants what Christine can offer him. The love and dependence of a young girl. A fresh, curious, talented soul with beautiful voice and bright eyes. She's the sun in his dark life, the sun he longs for, but also burns him so. He wants to be seen, but he hates to be seen. He disgusts his own soul, but he hopes so desperately someone else could see it and love him for it. Christine does see him, his face and his soul, and Phantom doesn't understand that it's his soul that scares Christine away, not his face. He's scarred in his heart first, before his face. His face doesn't matter, not to Christine, not to the audience, but to himself. He's lonely, he's pathetic, he's the victim of the world's cruelty, and all he wants is to be loved, but all he can do is to hide in the darkness and pour all his feelings into his music, and hope without hope that one day someone can see him, can sing his song. And when young Christine sings his song, that's when he starts falling for her, that's when he starts seeing the hope, and it's that hope that almost destroys him. (I really need to re-read the original novel to see if there's more in the writing, from their POVs.) He mistakes his feeling and desperation for Christine for love, and he mistakes Christine's looking upon him for the reciprocated love, when all he ever really wants is the kindness, which is exactly what Christine shows him, near the end, that gentlest kiss, that light touch to put his hands around her waist, her permission for him to savor the moment. That's all she can offer, but that's enough for him to realize what he wants, how much he doesn't want to hurt Christine or ruin her happiness, how human he still is, how vulnerable and intrinsically not evil he still is. He's just tragic.

Only now, after the third time rewatching, does I notice that the most torn moment for Christine isn't the song near the end between the three of them in the dungeon, before she kisses Phantom and eventually gets the permission to escape with Raoul, it's not that. It's the moment when Raoul asks her to sing Phantom's new opera *Don Juan* and she says no, because Raoul wants to use her as bait to capture Phantom, and that's the moment she has to make a decision. Her dilemma regarding Phantom isn't about whether to love him, but whether to betray him. She knows if she sings, she's betraying Phantom, helping people to catch him, hurt him, and she'll lose her angel of music, her teacher, and ultimately, the comfort she gets after her father's death. It's the torn song she sings before her father's tomb. There's still so much grief in her that no one notices but herself, not even Phantom (who only exploits it), not even Raoul (who's ignorant about it). She can't sing it, because if she does, if Phantom is gone, she'll know her father's promise is also gone, there is no angel watching over her again, it's as if her father would stop watching over her again, it's like her father is really gone. She can't sing, because if she does, she'd be the one to kill her own memory of her father, making the grief so much worse and strong, and she can't bear it. But Raoul doesn't realize that. Raoul still persuades her to sing, for good reason, from his point of view. He's just trying to get rid of the menace for the theater, he doesn't want people get scared or hurt, he wants the theater just keep running and benefiting, just like a business that it's supposed to be, it's his business after all. He's good-hearted and for good reason, but he can't see the pain he puts Christine through when he asks her of that. And Christine, the good, kind, compassionate girl that she is, eventually abides. She suppressed her own pain, her own grief, the grief and knowledge that she'll lose her father once again and lose her teacher, her angel, her Phantom all at the same night. Who hears her pain in the song she sang in *Don Juan* that night? Certainly not the soldiers trying to shoot the Phantom from Box Five, not Raoul when he's too busy fighting this war with Phantom, not even Phantom himself when he's fighting the war back. The men are fighting their war, and the girl's heart is bleeding. Whoever wins, she lost. Whoever wins, they think she gets the one who loves her one way or another, but only she knows, she'd always pain for the one getting hurt.

And the moment she says yes to singing *Don Juan*, she already sees the ending. It's not a happy ending story. It just wouldn't be.
